# The exercise that actually prevents falls

*By Russ L'HommeDieu, DPT, ATP, CFPS | Home Safety Solutions*

The exercise that prevents falls combines strength training with balance work that truly challenges you, done standing, several times a week, for months. Walking is great for your heart, but walking alone does not reliably prevent falls. Done right, exercise is the single most effective fall prevention strategy there is.

## What the research shows

In 2024, the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force reviewed 37 studies with more than 16,000 older adults. Exercise cut the rate of falls by 15% and injurious falls by 16%. The Task Force gave exercise a Grade B recommendation for adults 65 and older at increased fall risk.

Programs that include a balance component work even better. Strength training alone or balance training alone is less effective than the two together.

## The dose that works

This is where most people fall short. The research points to:

- **At least 50 hours of training in total,** spread over 3 to 6 months.
- **Three or more sessions a week,** with exercises made harder as you improve, for at least 12 weeks.
- **Then keep going.** The benefits fade when the exercise stops.

Fifty hours sounds like a lot, but it builds steadily when you train three or more times a week and keep going for months.

## Why walking alone isn't enough

Lower doses of exercise, or walking by itself, do not reliably reduce falls. In people already at high risk, a walking-only program can even raise risk.

Walking still belongs in your week. The Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans suggest 150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity a week, muscle strengthening on 2 or more days, and balance activities. It also helps to get up and move every 1 to 2 hours instead of sitting for long stretches.

## What "challenging balance" means

Balance exercises must be done standing, and they must be hard enough that you have to work. Sitting exercises build strength but do not train balance. A good program includes:

- **Standing balance, from wide to narrow:** feet together, then one foot in front of the other, then one leg. Later, a therapist may add eyes closed or a soft surface.
- **Moving balance:** reaching, turning, stepping in every direction, figure eights, and stepping over objects.
- **Strength:** thighs, hips, calves, and back. The muscles on the sides of your hips matter a lot, because sideways falls raise hip fracture risk about sixfold.
- **Sit-to-stand practice,** stairs, and getting down to and up from the floor.
- **Doing two things at once:** walking while talking or carrying a cup, so balance holds up in real life.

Always do balance work next to a sturdy counter or with a helper until you are ready to let go.

## Match the exercise to the person

- **Active and steady:** faster sit-to-stands, step-ups, squats, obstacle courses, and walking on grass or gravel. Even elite athletes train balance and reaction.
- **Frail or using a walker:** seated marches and band exercises for strength, plus supported standing work like holding the counter and mini squats.
- **Memory problems:** the same routine at the same time each day, with simple cues and music.

## Getting started safely

If you have fallen, feel unsteady, or have health conditions, start with your doctor or a physical therapist. A therapist can test your balance and prescribe a program such as the Otago Exercise Program, a home program built for older adults at high risk. The American Physical Therapy Association warns against under-dosed strength programs for older adults, so expect your program to feel like real work.

A simple first step is the CDC's [Chair Rise Exercise (PDF)](https://www.cdc.gov/steadi/pdf/steadi-brochure-chairriseex-508.pdf).
