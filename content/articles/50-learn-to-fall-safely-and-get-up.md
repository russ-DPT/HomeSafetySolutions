# Can you learn to fall safely and get up again?

*By Russ L'HommeDieu, DPT, ATP | Home Safety Solutions*

Yes. You can learn how to land in a way that protects your bones, and you can practice a step-by-step way to get up from the floor. Both skills should be learned with a therapist or a trained helper, practiced only when it is safe and supervised, and started on a mat or a bed, not a hard floor.

## Why this matters

Even with the best plan, some falls still happen. Part of fall prevention is making a fall less harmful: a safer landing, a faster rescue and the skill to get up.

Getting up is a big deal. One in eight older adults who fall lie on the floor for more than an hour, and up to 80% of people over 90 cannot get up on their own. A long lie can bring dehydration, kidney injury, pressure injury, hypothermia (a dangerous drop in body temperature), pneumonia and a loss of confidence. Our earlier article, [I've fallen and I can't get up](/ive-fallen-and-i-cant-get-up), covers what to do in that moment. This article covers how to prepare.

## Falling more safely

These techniques come from fall prevention training. A therapist can adapt them to your body and your health.

- **Tuck and roll.** Let your body rotate as you go down, so you land on large, padded areas like the buttocks and thigh.
- **Squat into a backward fall.** Bending your knees lowers you closer to the ground before you land.
- **Do not catch yourself on a stiff, straight arm.** That reaction is natural, but it can break a wrist.
- **Avoid landing straight on your side when you can.** Falling sideways raises the chance of a hip fracture about sixfold. Strong hip muscles help you stay upright when you start to tip sideways.

Some therapists adapt martial arts break-fall techniques with great care. Always practice on a mat or bed first, with someone beside you.

## Getting up from the floor, step by step

The usual sequence looks like this:

1. **Stay still and check for injury.** Take a breath. Do not rush to stand.
2. **Roll onto your side.**
3. **Push up to your hands and knees.**
4. **Crawl to a sturdy chair** or another solid piece of furniture.
5. **Bring one foot forward** so you are in a half-kneel.
6. **Push up and turn to sit** on the chair. Rest before you stand.

Therapists often teach this backward, starting with the last step. You first practice going from kneeling to sitting in a chair. Once that feels easy, you add the step before it, and so on, until you can start from lying on the floor. This builds success at each stage.

## If you cannot get up

- Call for help.
- Keep warm. Pull a blanket, towel or clothing over you if you can reach it.
- Shift your position from time to time.
- Stay as comfortable as you can until help arrives.

This is why a phone should be on your body, not across the room. A medical alert button (pendant or wrist, ideally with automatic fall detection) can shorten a long lie, but many older adults wait too long to press it. Practice pressing it so it feels normal. Technology helps, but it never replaces a person checking in.

## Who should practice, and how

Practice only when it is safe and supervised. That is especially true if you have osteoporosis, a recent fracture or surgery, dizziness, heart or blood pressure problems, or trouble with balance. Ask your doctor first, and learn from a physical or occupational therapist. In therapy, a floor transfer test checks whether you can get down to and up from the floor, which helps spot who is at risk of a long lie.

## A note for family caregivers

If your parent falls and is not hurt, do not drag or lift them. Coach them through the steps above instead, and bring a chair close. Lifting can injure both of you.

If you ever have to call the fire department just to help someone up (a "lift assist"), treat that as a warning sign. It is a reason to ask the doctor for a full falls assessment.

## Getting unstuck

Some people freeze in place or feel their balance slipping while standing. The "5 S" method helps:

- **Stop.**
- **Stand tall.**
- **Shake it off.**
- **Shift your weight.**
- **Step forward.**
