Bathroom Grab Bars: Placement, Anchoring and Blocking

By Russ L'HommeDieu, DPT, ATP Doctor of Physical Therapy and RESNA Assistive Technology Professional October 13, 2026 14 min read

A grab bar is only as strong as what is behind the wall. The bar you can see, shiny and well placed, does nothing if its screws are holding onto half an inch of drywall. The best grab bar installation starts before the wall is closed: solid wood blocking, set at the right height, with a written record of exactly where it is.

This article covers where grab bars belong in a bathroom, how each anchoring method compares, why solid wood blocking is the gold standard, and how to document the blocking so the next person who drills into that wall finds it. For a shorter overview of which bars most people need, see What grab bars do I actually need in the bathroom?

Why this matters

The bathroom is where falls at home tend to happen. A CDC study counted about 234,000 emergency room visits in one year for bathroom injuries among people 15 and older. About 81 percent were falls, and roughly two-thirds happened at the tub or shower. Injury rates were highest in people 85 and older.

When someone slips, they grab the nearest thing. If that is a grab bar anchored into framing, it holds. If it is a bar held by plastic drywall anchors, a towel bar or a shower door, it can tear out of the wall at the worst possible moment, and a person who trusted it falls harder than one who never had it.

Placement: where the bars go

The 2010 ADA Standards set grab bar rules for public and commercial restrooms. They do not govern a private home, but they are the best published reference for size and position, and a good starting point for a home plan.

LocationADA reference placementWhat it is for at home
Toilet, side wallBar 42 in long minimum, starting 12 in maximum from the rear wall and extending 54 in minimum in front of the toiletPushing up to stand and lowering to sit
Toilet, rear wallBar 36 in long minimum, extending at least 12 in on one side of the toilet centerline and 24 in on the otherExtra support for weaker users; often optional at home
Tub, back wallTwo parallel bars about 24 in long: one at 33 to 36 in, a lower one 8 to 10 in above the tub rimSitting, standing and reaching from inside the tub
Tub, control (faucet) endBar about 24 in long at the front edge of the tubSteadying while turning and stepping in
Tub, head endBar about 12 in long near the front edgeSupport at the far end
Transfer shower (36 x 36 in)Bars on the wall opposite the seat and on the back wallTransferring to and from the shower seat
Shower or tub entryNot an ADA requirementA vertical bar at the entry for stepping over the tub wall or curb; the bar I recommend most often

The same rules apply to every bar:

  • Height: 33 to 36 in above the floor, measured to the top of the gripping surface (except the lower tub bar)
  • Diameter: 1 1/4 to 2 in for round bars
  • Wall clearance: exactly 1 1/2 in between the bar and the wall, so a hand or forearm cannot get trapped
  • Clear space: nothing projecting within 12 in above the bar
  • Strength: the bar, fasteners, mounting and supporting structure must withstand 250 lb of force at any point, in any direction

Why a home plan starts with the person

A public restroom has to work for everyone. A home bathroom has to work for the people who live there. A tall person who pulls up from the toilet may need the bar higher and farther forward. A shorter person who pushes down needs it lower. Someone with a weak right side needs the bar on the left. A person who sits on a tub bench needs bars they can reach while seated.

That is why I watch the person move before I specify a single bar: how they stand from the toilet, step into the tub, turn in the shower and reach for support. Then each bar gets a length, orientation, height and position written down for the installer. It is also why the wall behind the bar should be ready for more than one possible position. That is where blocking comes in.

Anchoring options, from best to worst

Every grab bar has two or three mounting flanges, each held by screws. The question is what those screws bite into.

RankMethodHow it holdsWhen it fits
1Solid wood blockingEvery screw goes into solid lumber fastened between the studsNew construction or any renovation that opens the wall
2Continuous plywood backingScrews go into a plywood sheet fastened across the studs behind the finished wallRenovation where you want the most flexibility in bar position
3Wall studsScrews go into the edge of a wood studExisting walls, but only when the studs line up with where the bar is needed
4Grab bar anchors rated for the wallA heavy-duty anchor, rated by its manufacturer for grab bars and that wall type, spreads the load behind the wallExisting finished walls where a stud is not in the right place
5Plastic drywall anchors or toggle bolts not rated for grab barsGrip the back of the drywallNever for a grab bar
6Suction cupsPress against tile or glassNever for holding body weight

Why studs are not enough on their own

Studs are usually 16 in apart, but the right bar position rarely matches the framing. A 24 in bar can catch two studs. A bar on a vertical line at the tub entry may land between them. A 42 in toilet bar has to start within 12 in of the rear wall, and the stud may be in the wrong place. Installers end up moving the bar to fit the studs instead of the person, or putting one end into a stud and the other into an anchor. Either way, the person loses.

Tile, fiberglass and acrylic surrounds

These need extra care. Tile must be drilled with the right bit and sealed so water does not get behind it. Many one-piece fiberglass and acrylic tub surrounds have a gap between the shell and the wall framing, so a screw can crack the shell or pull through it. Some manufacturers make surrounds with built-in backing for grab bars. If the surround has no backing and nothing solid is behind it, the safe options are blocking during a remodel or a mounting system made for that surround.

The gold standard: solid wood blocking

Solid wood blocking is lumber, usually 2x6, 2x8 or 2x10, cut to fit between the studs and fastened to them, flush with the face of the framing, before drywall or cement board goes up. Once it is in, a grab bar can be screwed in anywhere across the blocked area, at whatever height and angle the person needs, with every screw in solid wood.

Blocking is the gold standard for three reasons:

  1. Strength. Every screw holds in solid lumber that is tied into the framing. That is how a bar meets the 250 lb standard reliably, at every flange.
  2. Freedom of placement. The bar goes where the person needs it, not where a stud happens to be.
  3. Future-proofing. A bar can be added, moved or replaced years later without opening the wall.

The standards recognize this. The ADA Standards let grab bars in dwelling units be installed later as long as the walls are properly reinforced for them. The Fair Housing Act requires reinforcement in the bathroom walls of covered multifamily units built for first occupancy after March 13, 1991, so grab bars can be added when needed.

When to install it

The only time blocking is cheap is when the wall is open. That means new construction, a bathroom remodel, a tub-to-shower conversion, or any repair that removes drywall or tile. If the wall is coming down anyway, block it, even if no one in the house needs a grab bar today. It is a small cost during framing and a large one later.

Where to block: my recommendation

These zones go beyond the ADA minimums on purpose, so the bar can be placed for the person rather than a code drawing. Heights are measured from the finished floor.

AreaBlocking zone
Toilet, side wallHorizontal band about 30 to 40 in high, from the rear wall corner to at least 54 in out
Toilet, rear wallHorizontal band about 30 to 40 in high, the full width behind the toilet
Tub, back wallFull length of the tub, from about 6 in above the rim up to about 40 in above the floor, covering both the upper bar and the lower bar 8 to 10 in above the rim
Tub and shower, entry edgeVertical band about 16 in wide at the entry, from about 30 in up to about 72 in, for a vertical bar
Tub, control and head end wallsBand from the tub rim to about 40 in high, from the front edge of the tub back at least 24 in
Shower, all wallsHorizontal band about 30 to 40 in high on every wall, plus the seat wall at seat height if a fold-down seat may be added
Beside the vanity or doorwayOptional band where a person may reach for support, often the spot where people grab a towel bar today

A few details I ask installers to follow:

  • Use straight, dry lumber, fastened securely to the studs at each end, flush with the stud face so the wall finish lies flat
  • Where a bar may land on a vertical line, add vertical blocking or a wider band
  • Keep plumbing supply lines, drains and valves out of the blocking zone where possible, and note exactly where they are
  • On wide areas, continuous 3/4 in plywood fastened across the studs is a good alternative that gives the same freedom of placement

Florida homes with concrete block walls

Many Tampa Bay homes have bathrooms on an exterior wall of concrete block, with furring strips and drywall over it. Wood blocking is not used there in the same way. A bar on a block wall is anchored into the masonry with fasteners rated for it, and the hollow cells in block are weaker than the solid webs. During a remodel, ask the contractor how the bar locations will get a solid anchor, and document the answer the same way.

Document the blocking, or it is lost

Blocking that no one can find is almost as bad as no blocking. Once the drywall and tile go up, it is invisible. Five years later, a new owner, a handyman or a family member installing a bar after a fall has no idea where it is. They guess, miss, and fall back on drywall anchors. The documentation is what turns blocking into a lasting feature of the house.

What to record

Before the wall is closed, capture:

  1. Photos of every open wall. Shoot each wall straight on, with a tape measure stretched across it and another running up from the floor, so the numbers are readable in the picture.
  2. Measurements from fixed points. For each block, record the distance from a corner or the toilet centerline, and the top and bottom heights from the finished floor. Note whether the floor height is to the subfloor or the finished floor, since tile can add an inch or more.
  3. Plumbing and wiring. Mark supply lines, drains, shower valves and any electrical runs, so no one drills into them.
  4. Materials. Lumber size, plywood thickness and how it was fastened.
  5. Who and when. Contractor name, date and permit number if there was one.

Turn it into a blocking map

Put it on one page per bathroom: a simple drawing of each wall with the blocked zones shaded and dimensioned, the plumbing marked, and the photos attached. A simple sketch is enough if the measurements are clear.

Keep it with the house

  • Keep a printed copy with the home's important papers, and a digital copy with the photos in cloud storage
  • Tape a copy inside the vanity door or the access panel behind the tub, where an installer will look
  • Give a copy to family members who help with the house
  • Pass it to the buyer if the home is sold; it is a selling point for an accessible home

Already closed the wall without a record?

A stud finder will locate studs but often cannot tell blocking from a stud or a pipe. A small inspection hole in a spot the bar flange will cover, or a check from behind through a closet or access panel, can confirm what is there before drilling. If nothing solid is found, use anchors rated for grab bars and that wall type.

Installation and inspection checklist

Use this when a bar goes in, and once a year after that.

Before installing

  • Bar position is set for the person who will use it, not just copied from a kit or a code drawing
  • Every screw location lands in blocking, plywood backing, a stud, or a rated anchor for that wall type
  • Plumbing and wiring locations are confirmed clear
  • Bar is rated by its manufacturer to hold body weight, 1 1/4 to 2 in in diameter, with a textured or knurled grip for wet hands

After installing

  • Bar is 1 1/2 in from the wall and nothing projects within 12 in above it
  • Tile and surround penetrations are sealed against water
  • A firm pull and push in every direction produces no movement, flex or creaking
  • The blocking map is updated with the new bar location

Every year

  • No loose flanges, turning bars, cracked tile or caulk gaps at the screws
  • The bars still fit the person; needs change after a surgery, a stroke or a new mobility aid
