What's the difference between a ramp, a threshold ramp and a lift?

By Russ L'HommeDieu, DPT, ATP Doctor of Physical Therapy and RESNA Assistive Technology Professional September 29, 2026 9 min read

A threshold ramp smooths a lip of an inch or two, such as a door sill. A full ramp replaces one or more steps and needs a lot of length. A lift carries the person up vertically or along the stairs and fits where a ramp will not. The right choice depends on how high the rise is, how much space you have, and how the person moves.

Threshold ramps

These small wedges, usually rubber or aluminum, bridge a raised door sill or sliding door track. They are one of the most useful low-cost changes for walker, rollator and wheelchair users.

For public buildings, the ADA says a change in level up to 1/4 inch needs no treatment. From 1/4 to 1/2 inch it must be beveled. Anything over 1/2 inch must be treated as a ramp. At home, the goal is simpler: no edge that catches a wheel, a walker leg or a toe. The ramp must match the exact height of the sill.

Full ramps

The slope rule

The ADA standard for public buildings is a slope no steeper than 1:12. That means 1 inch of rise for every 12 inches of ramp, or about 1 foot of ramp for every inch of height.

Gentler is better when you have room

1:12 is the steepest allowed, not the best. The U.S. Access Board notes that gentler slopes work for more people. Rehabilitation guidance points out that some people find 1:12 hard and do better at 1:16 or 1:20. That matters for anyone self-propelling a manual wheelchair or a caregiver pushing one.

Other ramp basics

  • Level landings at least 60 inches long at the top, bottom and any turns
  • At least 36 inches of clear width
  • Handrails on both sides when the rise is more than 6 inches, 34 to 38 inches high
  • Edge protection so wheels cannot roll off the side
  • A slip-resistant surface that drains, which matters in Florida rain

Modular aluminum ramps can be installed quickly and removed later. Wood and concrete ramps are permanent and may need permits.

How to choose

  1. Rent if the need is temporary. After a joint replacement or a hospital stay, a rented modular ramp can be installed quickly and removed later. Amramp says installations can happen in as little as one day, with removal included.
  2. Buy modular if the need is long term. Aluminum modular ramps do not rot, can be taken apart and moved, and can be extended later.
  3. Do not choose a ramp by the number of steps. Choose by the rise in inches and the slope the person can manage. A portable ramp listed for "one to two steps" may be far steeper than is safe for a walker user or someone pushing a wheelchair alone.
  4. Count the landings. A ramp needs a level landing at the top, the bottom and every turn. Platforms add most of the cost to a modular system.
  5. Ask about permits and your HOA. Ask the installer whether your city or county requires a permit, and check whether your homeowners association must approve an exterior ramp.

Brands are listed as examples. Home Safety Solutions does not sell ramps and accepts no payments from ramp companies or installers. The Ramp Calculator at ramp.engineer is our own free tool.

Lifts

  • Vertical platform lifts work like a small open elevator. The person rolls or walks on, and the platform rises. They fit where a ramp would be too long.
  • Stair lifts carry a seated person along an indoor staircase. The person must be able to transfer on and off the seat safely at both ends.
  • Both need power, so plan for battery backup during outages.

Who pays

Original Medicare does not pay for ramps, stair lifts or home elevators. Medicare's national equipment list classifies elevators and stairway elevators as convenience items. Some Medicare Advantage plans, Florida Medicaid's long-term care program and VA grants may help with some home modifications.
