Understanding Fall Risk and Fall Prevention

A Home Safety Solutions white paper on why older adults fall, how risk is found, and what actually prevents falls.

By Russ L'HommeDieu, DPT, ATP, CFPS Doctor of Physical Therapy, RESNA Assistive Technology Professional, Certified Fall Prevention Specialist November 2, 2026 White paper, 43 pages

Download the white paper (PDF) Take the fall risk quick check

Executive summary

Falls are the leading cause of injury and injury death in Americans 65 and older, and most are preventable when risk is found early and addressed across the person, the medications and the home. More than 14 million older adults (about 1 in 4) report a fall each year, 43,020 died from falls in 2024, and fall injuries sent more than 3.85 million to emergency departments in 2023 (National Safety Council; the CDC's estimate, which counts differently, is about 4.5 million a year).

Key messages

  • Falls are multifactorial. About a third of the drivers are intrinsic (body and health), a third are medications and alcohol, and a third are environmental. Most falls happen when several risks combine, so single fixes rarely solve the problem.
  • Exercise is the strongest single intervention. Progressive balance, functional and strength training reduces the rate of falls by about 15% in pooled trials and is a USPSTF Grade B recommendation (2024). Programs need roughly 50 hours over 3 to 6 months and must continue, or the benefit fades.
  • High-risk people need a multifactorial assessment and a tailored plan. The 2022 World Falls Guidelines and CDC STEADI agree: screen everyone, assess those at risk, and intervene on what is found.
  • Home modification works best for those at highest risk and when delivered by a trained clinician. The guidelines favor assessment of the person's capacities and behaviors in their own home, not a generic checklist alone.
  • Medications are the most modifiable risk. Sedatives, sleep aids, antidepressants, antipsychotics, opioids, gabapentinoids, anticholinergics and blood pressure drugs all raise risk. Three or more central nervous system active drugs together is a red flag (2023 AGS Beers Criteria).
  • Prepare for the fall that still happens. Learning how to get up from the floor, keeping a phone or alert device on the body, and protecting bone health reduce the harm of falls that are not prevented.

Start with a screen

The paper recommends that every adult 65 and older be asked about falls at least once a year and after any fall, because most people will not raise it themselves. Screening begins with three key questions: Have you fallen in the past year? Do you feel unsteady when standing or walking? Are you worried about falling? A yes to any of them leads to the 12-item Stay Independent checklist, where a score of 4 or more means increased risk.
